Finding the Right Moments: a Local Framework for Question Answering on Long Videos

Sayed Ilham Azhar Harun · project local-surf, branch framework-2 · October 2026 · everything runs on one 16 GB consumer GPU with small, frozen open models

The problem. A person asks a question about a long video (a lecture, a vlog, a film; 5 to 60 minutes). A small vision-language model can only look at a few pictures. The framework must find the few moments that contain the answer, using the video's speech and its pictures, and give the model the right text. Below is the best-working framework found by the experiments in Part 2; arrows show the order of work.

Part 1 · The best-working framework

Input: the question (with its answer options), the video, and its timed transcriptTranscript from the video's subtitles, or from our Whisper speech recognition when there are none.
▼ find where the speech talks about the question
1 · Transcript search (BM25)The transcript is cut into 20-second pieces; word matching with the question and options picks the 4 best pieces (+ neighbours).
▼ two paths propose candidate pictures in parallel
2a · Path A: speech path — HEAD A, the speech-to-moment localiserHead AScores every transcript line (not only the BM25 pieces) with two frozen text models (MiniLM, BGE) plus a small learned layer that reads the context of nearby lines and the type of question; several relevant lines then vote for a moment. Finds the evidence moment far better than word matching: 36.6% vs 28.0% of questions hit by its 6 moments (confirmed, +8.5 points). In the best-accuracy setting the simpler zero-shot speech rule (MiniLM inside the BM25 pieces) sits here; Head A is the drop-in that localises better.
2b · Path B: picture path (hybrid visual search)Half of its frames come from a scan inside the BM25 pieces, half from a dense scan of the whole video (a picture every 2 s, ranked by MobileCLIP similarity to the question). This is the change that matters most on long videos: the right picture is now among the candidates for 60% of questions instead of 33%.
▼ merge, remove near-duplicates
3 · Candidate pool: about 10–12 framesEach frame keeps its time, which path found it, and its picture embedding.
▼ choose the final 4 frames as a set
4 · HEAD B, the frame-set selectorHead BPicks the 4 frames that best match the question and its answer options, while skipping near-identical frames (MMR rule). Learned versions were also built and tested: an evidence scorer, a scorer trained on the answer model's own feedback, an option-evidence set rule, and answer-confidence selection (the answer model answers with 4 candidate sets and the most confident answer is kept; best of the learned variants, +0.9 points on test, not confirmed).
▼ give the model the speech that matters
5 · Speech for the answer model (up to 600 words)The exact speech around the 4 chosen frames, plus the best transcript lines found by combining word matching with meaning-based matching. Keeps 68% of the speech near the evidence; the v2 excerpt kept only 28%.
▼
Output: the frozen answer model (Qwen3-VL-2B or 4B) reads the 4 frames + the speech and answersThe answer model is never trained; only the steps above decide what it sees.
Headline results (locked test, 459 questions never used before). With the 4B answer model the full framework scores 55.6% versus 50.3% for the starting v2 framework (+5.2 points, confirmed), and keeping the right speech adds +2.8, confirmed. With the 2B model, on long videos (CG-Bench) the tuned framework scores 50.9% versus 34.5% (+16.4, confirmed), and overall it beats the two standard frame-selection rules (+4.6 and +5.0, confirmed).
What the heads add, honestly. Head A clearly improves finding the evidence moment and puts the right picture into the pool more often (+4.9 points, confirmed), but the small answer model did not turn this into more correct answers; the frozen test kept the simpler speech rule in the best-accuracy setting. Head B's learned versions found evidence frames more often on long-video test questions (51–56% vs 47%) but did not raise accuracy; the tuned MMR rule remains the selector of the best configuration.

Part 2 · The research journey, step by step

Each step below says what was built, how it was tested, the result, and what was decided next. Percentages are the share of questions answered correctly unless stated otherwise. “Confirmed” means the 95% interval of the difference does not include zero.

Step 1 · Version 1 (branch QAframework), September 2026

Version 1: a cost-aware controller that decides when to look at the video

transcript → BM25 windows → candidate moments → decode frames on demand → MobileCLIP “scout” → controller (look / expand / stop, gain minus cost) → frozen Qwen3-VL-2B answers

Research idea tested: train the controller to look at the video exactly when important speech is damaged or missing. Result: not supported. On LongVideoBench, damaging the important speech changed the answer in only 13% (train) / 4% (dev) of cases, so there was almost nothing to learn; no trained variant looked more often under targeted damage than under control damage.

Benchmark of the working v1 system (440 LongVideoBench questions, 252 videos, 2B model, up to 8 frames):

Frame strategyMultiple choiceOpen-ended (local judge)
Transcript only (no frames)32.5%44.1%
Evenly spaced frames + transcript39.3%33.4%
Transcript-retrieved moments36.4%32.0%
MobileCLIP top frames41.6%32.3%
Cost-aware controller37.5%29.8%

Decision: the visual similarity rule was the best v1 strategy (+9.1 over transcript only, confirmed). The controller idea was closed; work moved to a new design with two proposal paths (Version 2).

Data — LongVideoBench (public benchmark, NeurIPS 2024). 3,763 videos (8 s to 60 min) with 6,678 human-written multiple-choice questions and aligned subtitles. We used 440 questions of its public validation set. Limits: a subset, not the full benchmark; it was also used while developing v1, so it is not an untouched test. The open-ended column uses a local judge model (Phi-4-mini) whose agreement with humans was never measured, and LongVideoBench is a multiple-choice benchmark, so its open-ended numbers compare strategies only.
Step 2 · Version 2 (branch framework-2), early October 2026

Version 2: two proposal paths, Head A (speech credit) and Head B (frame-set selector)

transcript → BM25 windows → Path A (speech lines scored by MiniLM, frame at line end) + Path B (visual scan inside the windows) → pool (~11 frames) → Head A / Head B learned selectors → 4 frames + 120-word BM25 transcript excerpt → frozen 2B answer model

Main experiment (197 lecture questions, 20 MIT lectures, each lecture tested by heads that never saw it):

Selector (4 frames)Accuracy
Transcript only15.2%
MobileCLIP top-434.5%
MobileCLIP + diversity36.0%
Head B, learned (with screen text)34.7%
Head A, learned frame credit30.5% (significantly worse)
Zero-shot transcript relevance (no training)38.6%

Follow-ups in v2: a pilot (132 questions) and a “fourth frame” completion study (−1.0, refuted) found no learned gain; spreading the visual scan evenly across windows gave +4.6 (not confirmed) and did not transfer to LongVideoBench (43.0% vs 42.5%); option-rotation scoring was added after an answer-position bias was found. Decision: the learned heads did not beat simple rules on this data; v3 had to start from credible public benchmarks where pictures provably matter.

Data — self-made MIT lecture questions. Multiple-choice questions were generated from 20 MIT OpenCourseWare lectures (Gilbert Strang) by a larger model (Qwen3-VL-4B), checked automatically, and questions the 2B model could answer without any evidence were dropped (349 verified → 197 kept). Possible flaws: the questions are synthetic and machine-checked, not human-written; the generator put the correct answer at A or B in 171 of 197 kept questions, and the dropping rule interacted with the answer model's letter preference, which pushed “transcript only” below chance (15.2%); we corrected this with option-rotation scoring, but the set remains development data, not an independent benchmark.
Step 3 · v3 gates: which benchmarks can show an effect?

Checking three benchmarks before building anything

A frame-selection method can only be judged where pictures change the answer, so each benchmark was first checked with simple rules (same 2B model):

Data — EduVidQA (EMNLP 2025). 296 lecture videos with 5,234 open-ended questions, scored by FactQA (an AI judge counts supported facts; we used the paper's prompt word for word, with a free NVIDIA-hosted model). Limits found: the transcript alone already supports about half of the reference answers' facts, frames made the small model's answers shorter and worse, and 10 of 87 caption tracks labelled English were actually Tamil (filtered out). The 269 expert-written real questions were kept locked and never used.
Data — Video-MMMU (2025). 300 lecture videos from university subjects, 3 questions per video (Perception, Comprehension, Adaptation), mostly 10-option multiple choice. Perception questions often require reading text on the screen. We used Perception and Comprehension, with our own Whisper transcripts. Limits: the benchmark has no official training split, so we split its videos once by a fixed rule (one third dev, two thirds test).
Data — CG-Bench (2024). 1,219 long videos (10–80 minutes), 12,129 questions; for every question, human annotators marked the seconds where the answer is visible (“clue intervals”), which lets us measure directly whether a method found the right moment. We used the 51 English-subtitled videos available to us (510 questions), split by video into train 37 / dev 8 / test 6. Limits: the test part is small (55 questions on 6 videos, so its intervals are wide), the split is our own (the benchmark has none), and three open-ended reference answers are broken in the dataset (counted as wrong for every method).

Part 3 · What can and cannot be claimed

Supported by the locked test. On long videos, searching only where the transcript matches the question misses most of the visual evidence; adding a dense whole-video visual search, matching frames to the question and its answer options, and giving the model the speech around the chosen frames improves a local 2B/4B system substantially over the starting design and over standard frame-selection rules. Keeping the right speech helps a stronger reader.

Not supported. That the learned heads raise final accuracy: Head A finds the evidence moment significantly better and Head B's learned versions pick evidence frames more often on long videos, but neither turned this into more correct answers with these small answer models. These are reported as negative results, together with the original offset idea that did not help.

Why the absolute numbers are modest. The answer models are deliberately small (2B and 4B, frozen, one 16 GB GPU). Even when given the frames human annotators marked as the answer, the 2B model reaches only 63% on CG-Bench dev; the framework's gains are measured against other ways of choosing frames for the same model.
